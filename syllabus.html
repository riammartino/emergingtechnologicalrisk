Emerging Technological Risk:
Deciding Under Deep Uncertainty

Six units, one decision. A hands-on course that takes senior leaders from "how does this technology actually work?" to a defensible, joint-process risk-acceptance decision they can own — across AI and agentic AI, the post-quantum transition, autonomous systems, space and spectrum, and the digital foundation beneath all of it.

Overview

Senior leaders increasingly have to accept risk from technologies that can't be fully tested, change faster than policy, and fail in unfamiliar ways. This course is built to make that decision well. It is deliberately short and hands-on: every unit gives you just enough of how the technology works to see how it is threatened, then what mitigates the threat, then how you decide — and everything you do feeds one capstone decision you brief at the end.

The through-line: threat → mitigation → decision

Each technology is a case of the same problem, so each unit follows the same four beats:

  • Foundation. A plain-language explanation and a short, no-jargon demo of how the technology actually works. You cannot judge a risk you do not understand.
  • Threat. How it fails and how an adversary turns it against you — shown, not just described.
  • Mitigation. The controls, standards, and policy that actually reduce the risk.
  • Implementation & decision. What you require from a program, and what you add to your capstone decision.

An innovation-strategy lens

The course borrows a few durable constructs from technology-strategy scholarship (Schilling, Strategic Management of Technological Innovation) to explain why these decisions are hard — used as framing, not as a separate unit:

  • S-curves — where a technology sits in its maturity, and why the "when" is unknowable (Units 1, 3).
  • Competence-destroying vs. -enhancing change — why post-quantum is a wholesale replacement, not a patch (Unit 3).
  • Architectural vs. modular innovation — why new tech breaks large enterprise architectures on integration (Unit 5).
  • Make / buy / ally — the commercial-vs-military sourcing decision (Unit 5).
  • Her short opening-case-per-chapter format — adopted as one digestible case per unit.
What changed in this draft (0.2). Restructured from nine modules to six units with a single build-up capstone; every unit now leads with a foundation and a short hands-on sandbox; added space & spectrum, the digital foundation (cyber, cloud, data centers & power, chips, OT/ICS), human factors, GenAI deception, and AI catastrophic-misuse governance; rebuilt the decision unit on joint risk methodology; refreshed the threat literature to 2024–2026, including the first reported agentic-AI cyber-espionage case.

Learning outcomes

1Frame technological uncertainty. Tell measurable risk from deep uncertainty, and pick the method that fits — NIST SP 800-30 determination versus robust decision-making, adaptive pathways, and premortems.
2Understand each technology well enough to interrogate it. Explain, in plain terms, how AI/agents, cryptography, autonomy, space/spectrum, and the digital foundation work — because you demoed each one.
3See the threats testing cannot reveal. Evasion, poisoning, prompt injection and excessive agency; harvest-now-decrypt-later; specification gaming; PNT denial; supply-chain, cloud and OT compromise; automation bias.
4Apply the current standards and policy baseline. NIST AI RMF and AI 100-2; FIPS 203/204/205 and the 2026 PQC deadlines; DoDD 3000.09; SP 800-82 (OT), 800-161 (supply chain), 800-145 (cloud); NSPM-11 and EO 14409/14412.
5Weigh commercial against military use. Data rights, integration and reverse compatibility into enterprise architectures, and the hard problem of adapting commercial AI/autonomy — built for predictable, data-rich life — to data-sparse, unpredictable combat.
6Own a joint-process decision. Produce and brief a risk-acceptance recommendation with risk-to-mission and risk-to-force, range-based likelihood, mitigations, signposts, and a named accepting authority.

How it works

Six units. Each unit is about 90 minutes of contact time and produces one section of your capstone. There is no separate reading load beyond the short essentials and one optional media piece per unit; the sandbox and the case are the work.

One 90-minute session per week (in person or virtual) plus ~30 minutes to finish that week's capstone section. Sandboxes can be run live in pairs or beforehand.

Foundation + demo · ~25 min

Plain-language "how it works," anchored by a no-code demo (e.g., train a model in your browser, build a quantum circuit by dragging gates). You leave able to picture the mechanism.

Sandbox + case · ~50 min

A ~15-minute Colab sandbox that makes the threat and its mitigation tangible, then a short business-school-style case discussion.

Capstone step · ~15 min

Add this unit's section to your decision package. That is the assignment — nothing else to hand in.

The capstone — one decision, built across six units

Instead of six disconnected assignments, you build a single Technology Risk & Decision Package on one capability you care about — a system your organization is fielding, buying, or being asked to trust. You add one section per unit and finish with a joint-process risk-acceptance recommendation you brief in ten minutes.

The end state — a joint-process risk-acceptance recommendation

Section F is written the way the joint force actually decides on risk, not as a loose memo:

  • Joint Risk Analysis (CJCSM 3105.01): state risk to mission and risk to force separately, each rated probability × consequence, with an overall level and a recommended response.
  • Planning-process risk step (JPP/MDMP): identify hazards, assess, develop controls, decide, assign who supervises.
  • Authorizing-official acceptance (DoDI 8510.01) and the RIO construct: name who accepts the residual risk, the tolerance it sits within (NIST IR 8286), and what reopens the decision.

A full section-by-section template and the grading rubric are in the capstone worksheet distributed with the course (capstone.md).

1Foundations — Deciding Under Deep UncertaintyWhy risk tools break for emerging tech · robust decision-making · S-curves · the premortem▶

When you cannot estimate likelihood, what are you actually deciding?

Foundation + demo

How the risk "machinery" you already use is built — likelihood × impact, the RMF, the risk register — and the hidden assumption that likelihood is knowable. Knight's risk-vs-uncertainty distinction; robust decision-making (choose the option that fails least badly across many futures); the S-curve as a way to place a technology's maturity. Demo: a risk-matrix ranking that flips when one uncertain input changes.

Threat → mitigation → implementation

ThreatThe real hazard is deciding confidently on a number that is a guess with a label; over- and under-reaction both have costs.
MitigationElicit ranges, not points; robust strategies; adaptive pathways with signposts; premortems before signing.
ImplementationWrite likelihood as a range with its evidence basis; set a review date and the signals that would reopen the decision.
Sandbox · ~15 min notebook in the hands-on build
Move one uncertain input and watch the "best" option change; run a small robust-decision screen across many futures and read the regret. Colab · no code to write.
Capstone A — Frame & uncertainty: name your capability and the decision, classify what is knowable vs. deeply uncertain, place it on the S-curve, list first signposts.

Case (Schilling-style)

A technology-adoption decision under deep uncertainty — the post-quantum transition as a competence-destroying shift leaders must act on before the threat is provable.

Essentials + media

  • NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1, ch. 2–3 csrc.nist.gov FINAL
  • NIST IR 8286, §§2–3 (risk appetite & tolerance) csrc.nist.gov FINAL
  • Lempert, Popper & Bankes, Shaping the Next One Hundred Years (RAND, 2003), ch. 1–2 free PDF
  • Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," HBR (2007) hbr.org
  • Schilling, Strategic Management of Technological Innovation — S-curves & technology maturity (framing)
  • Optional media: a short robust-decision-making explainer (instructor selects & click-tests).
2AI & Agentic AI — Threats and ControlsHow models and agents work · evasion, poisoning, injection, excessive agency · human factors · catastrophic-misuse governance▶

An agent's risk is set by what it can touch, not by how smart it is. What can yours touch?

Foundation + demo

What machine learning is (it learns patterns from data; every accuracy number is conditional on the test data) and what an agent is (a model wired to tools and given authority to act). Demo: train a classifier in the browser with your own examples (Google Teachable Machine) to see how data — and biased or poisoned data — shapes behavior, with no code.

Threat → mitigation → implementation

ThreatEvasion (adversarial inputs), poisoning/backdoors in training data, prompt injection, excessive agency; GenAI deception and synthetic media; automation bias in the human; and catastrophic-misuse (CBRN/cyber "uplift") at the frontier.
MitigationLeast privilege on tools, human approval gates, separated execution identity; red-teaming and evaluations; data provenance; calibration/out-of-distribution checks; content provenance (C2PA). Governed by the NIST AI RMF and AI 100-2, and frontier safety frameworks with capability thresholds.
ImplementationDemand a written authority statement (tools, permissions, oversight), a measured injection/eval result, and — for frontier models — evidence from misuse evaluations before fielding.
Sandbox · ~15 min notebook in the hands-on build
Give a small AI agent tools that change real (sandboxed) files, then watch a booby-trapped document hijack it — and watch least privilege and a human gate stop it. Colab + open-weight model · plus the no-code Lakera "Gandalf" injection game.
Capstone B — AI/agent threat & control: where AI/agents sit in your capability (or its supply chain or the adversary's hands), the exposures that apply, and the controls you will require.

Case (Schilling-style)

The first reported AI-orchestrated cyber-espionage campaign (GTG-1002, disclosed Nov 2025; MITRE Campaign C0062): a state-linked actor ran ~80–90% of an intrusion campaign against ~30 targets agentically, with humans at only 4–6 decision points, by decomposing the attack into "innocent" sub-tasks and posing as a defensive-testing firm. Taught defensively: where the agent's authority created the risk, and which controls would have blunted it.

Essentials + media

  • NIST AI 100-1 (AI RMF), Part 2 doi FINAL
  • NIST AI 100-2 E2025, Adversarial ML Taxonomy, §§1–3 (incl. prompt injection) doi FINAL
  • OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025) — LLM01 injection, LLM06 excessive agency genai.owasp.org
  • Anthropic, Disrupting the first reported AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign (2025) anthropic.com; MITRE ATT&CK Campaign C0062 attack.mitre.org
  • Human factors: Parasuraman & Riley, "Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse" (1997); Lee & See, "Trust in Automation" (2004)
  • Governance of catastrophic misuse: NSPM-11 (2026) whitehouse.gov IN FORCE; frontier-lab safety frameworks (Anthropic RSP; OpenAI Preparedness; DeepMind Frontier Safety)
  • Optional media: 3Blue1Brown, "But what is a neural network?" (≈19 min) youtube.
3Cryptography & Quantum — The Post-Quantum TransitionHow public-key crypto works and why quantum breaks it · harvest-now-decrypt-later · the 2026 deadlines · migration as a program▶

The threat is a date you cannot know; the exposure started when the recording did. What is your X, Y and Z?

Foundation + demo

What cryptography does (integrity, confidentiality, authenticity, key agreement), why public-key math is what quantum computers threaten (Shor) while symmetric keys mostly survive (Grover), and why post-quantum is competence-destroying — a wholesale replacement of RSA/ECC, not a patch. Demo: build a tiny quantum circuit by dragging gates (IBM Quantum Composer), then generate a post-quantum key and signature — no code.

Threat → mitigation → implementation

Threat"Harvest now, decrypt later": adversaries record encrypted traffic today to read it once a quantum computer exists. Forged signatures and certificates. Crypto is buried in everything, so exposure is broad.
MitigationThe NIST standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA); crypto-agility; hybrid key exchange during transition; a cryptographic bill of materials (CBOM); inventory first.
ImplementationPosition your capability against the 2026 deadlines: EO 14412 / OMB M-26-15 (2030 key establishment, 2031 signatures for high-value systems) and the DoW PQC Strategy (support by 2030) / CNSA 2.0 for NSS.
Sandbox · ~15 min notebook in the hands-on build
Generate post-quantum keys and a signature, compare their size to today's, and run the Mosca calculator (X data-secrecy + Y migration vs. Z time-to-quantum) on your own numbers. Colab + cryptography ≥48 · plus IBM Quantum Composer (no-code).
Capstone C — Cryptographic exposure: what your capability protects, its data secrecy horizon, your Mosca result, and where you stand against the deadlines.

Case (Schilling-style)

A program facing 2030/2031: sequencing a multi-year migration under a fixed deadline and an unknowable threat date — the archetypal competence-destroying transition.

Essentials + media

  • NIST FIPS 203/204/205 — read the overviews 203 · 204 · 205 FINAL
  • EO 14412 (22 Jun 2026) federalregister.gov and OMB M-26-15 (24 Jun 2026) whitehouse.gov IN FORCE
  • Department of War PQC Strategy (Apr 2026, released Jun 2026) dodcio.defense.gov IN FORCE; NSA CNSA 2.0 media.defense.gov IN FORCE (NSS)
  • NIST IR 8547 (ipd), transition timeline csrc.nist.gov DRAFT; Mosca (2018) doi
  • Brief: quantum sensing & PNT — NIST IR 8323 r1 (Foundational PNT Profile) csrc.nist.gov
  • Optional media: IBM, "What is quantum computing?" explainer ibm.com.
4Contested Domains — Autonomy, Space & SpectrumOperating-design-domain limits · human judgment · LEO, on-orbit links, 5G, contested spectrum · PNT denial▶

"A human will be in the loop." Is that physically possible for this system — and does it still work when the environment fights back?

Foundation + demo

How an autonomous system works (sense → decide → act, inside a defined operating-design-domain, at a physical time constant) and how the space/electromagnetic layer works (satellites in low-Earth orbit, inter-satellite/on-orbit links, ground segments, spectrum as a scarce contested resource, 5G, and position/navigation/timing). Demo: a control loop that holds inside its design domain and fails outside it; a look at how crowded LEO and the spectrum chart really are.

Threat → mitigation → implementation

ThreatAutonomy failing with physical consequence; specification gaming; adapting commercial autonomy (trained on predictable civilian data) to unpredictable combat; LEO congestion and ASAT; jamming and spoofing of links and GPS; permissive vs. non-permissive/denied spectrum.
MitigationA declared design domain and edge behavior; realistic human-on-the-loop time budgets; DoDD 3000.09 review; resilient/alternative PNT; proliferated-LEO and multi-orbit resilience; electromagnetic spectrum operations and management.
ImplementationRequire the design domain in writing and a defined action at its edge; test in degraded/denied conditions, not just nominal ones.
Sandbox · ~15 min notebook in the hands-on build
Run a stabilizing controller, then change the physics it never trained on and add sensor latency until control collapses — and compare the collapse point to human reaction time. Colab + Gymnasium · plus a live LEO/orbit viewer and the U.S. spectrum-allocation chart.
Capstone D — Physical & EM exposure: your capability's design-domain limits and its dependence on space, links, spectrum, and PNT — and how it behaves when those are contested.

Case (Schilling-style)

Fielding a commercial-derived autonomous or space capability into a non-permissive environment: what breaks when the data thins out and the spectrum is denied.

Essentials + media

  • DoDD 3000.09, Autonomy in Weapon Systems (2023) esd.whs.mil UNDER REVISION (update directed by NSPM-11)
  • DeepMind, "Specification gaming" (2020) deepmind.google; SAE J3016 (defines operating-design-domain)
  • Space Policy Directive-5, Cybersecurity Principles for Space Systems (2020) federalregister.gov; NIST IR 8270 (commercial satellite ops) csrc.nist.gov
  • NIST IR 8323 r1, Foundational PNT Profile csrc.nist.gov
  • Scharre, Army of None (2018), ch. 1–3 — human in / on / out of the loop
  • Optional media: a short LEO-congestion / on-orbit-communications explainer (instructor selects & click-tests).
5The Digital Foundation — Cyber, Cloud, Chips, Power & OTThe physical stack under the software · supply chain · cloud vs. data centers · chiponomics · OT/ICS · commercial-vs-military integration▶

Every capability in this course rests on chips, power, and someone else's cloud. What does yours quietly depend on, and who controls it?

Foundation + demo

How the digital stack rests on physical things: chips (where they are made, "chiponomics," export controls), data centers (power, cooling, physical and geographic concentration), cloud (the shared-responsibility model, multi-tenancy, concentration and lock-in — a distinct risk from the data centers underneath), and operational technology (the PLCs and industrial control systems that move physical things). Demo: generate a software bill of materials for a live system and audit it against the public vulnerability database.

Threat → mitigation → implementation

ThreatSoftware/hardware supply-chain compromise; cloud misconfiguration and shared-responsibility gaps; provider/geographic concentration; chip supply concentration and export-control shocks; data-center power and foreign dependency; OT/ICS attacks with physical effect (Stuxnet, Ukraine grid, Colonial Pipeline, Triton).
MitigationC-SCRM (SP 800-161), SBOM/CBOM on a risk basis (OMB M-26-05), zero trust, cloud impact levels and the Cloud SRG, OT segmentation and monitoring (SP 800-82). Integration seen as architectural innovation (Schilling); a deliberate make/buy/ally choice.
ImplementationCommercial-vs-military reality: tech data rights, reverse compatibility into legacy enterprise architectures, and adapting commercial AI/autonomy — built for data-rich, predictable life — to data-sparse, unpredictable combat.
Sandbox · ~15 min notebook in the hands-on build
Produce a software bill of materials for a running system, audit it live for known vulnerabilities, tally the licenses, and map a data type to the cloud impact level that decides where it may run. Colab + pip-audit · plus the CISA Known-Exploited-Vulnerabilities catalog and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS.
Capstone E — Dependency & integration: your capability's cyber, cloud, data-center/power, chip, and OT dependencies, plus the data-rights and integration reality of getting it into the enterprise.

Case (Schilling-style)

A commercial-technology integration decision — data rights and reverse compatibility — framed with make/buy/ally and architectural vs. modular innovation. Pair with a chip-supply shock (Chris Miller, Chip War) and a cloud-concentration lesson (the 2024 CrowdStrike outage).

Essentials + media

  • NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1 (C-SCRM), ch. 1–2 csrc.nist.gov FINAL
  • NIST SP 800-145 (definition of cloud) csrc.nist.gov and SP 800-144 (public-cloud security) csrc.nist.gov FINAL
  • NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 (OT security), exec summary csrc.nist.gov FINAL
  • OMB M-26-05 (risk-based software/hardware security; SBOM/attestation now optional) whitehouse.gov IN FORCE; DoD Cloud SRG (impact levels) public.cyber.mil
  • Miller, Chip War (2022), intro & conclusion; Schilling — architectural innovation & make/buy/ally (framing)
  • Optional media: a short data-center-power / chip-supply explainer (instructor selects & click-tests).
6Convergence & The DecisionWhere risks compound · joint risk analysis · risk-to-mission vs. risk-to-force · the documented, owned decision▶

At the intersection of any two of these technologies, who is accountable? If the answer is "several offices," it is nobody.

Foundation + demo

How risks compound where technologies meet (an agent holding credentials, controlling an autonomous system, on commercial data, whose signatures quantum will break), and how the joint force actually decides on risk — Joint Risk Analysis (CJCSM 3105.01), risk-to-mission vs. risk-to-force, probability × consequence, the planning-process risk step, and authorizing-official acceptance of residual risk. Demo: a convergence map that shows which node is most coupled — and least owned.

Threat → mitigation → implementation

ThreatThe largest strategic risks sit at the seams, where no single program office is accountable and no single test covers the interaction.
MitigationName an owner for each heavy edge; choose a robust risk-reduction posture across futures; write the decision down with signposts and a review date.
ImplementationProduce a joint-process risk-acceptance recommendation the accepting authority can sign.
Sandbox · ~15 min notebook in the hands-on build
Weight the convergence edges for your capability, then generate a risk-acceptance memorandum from a joint-risk-matrix worksheet (risk-to-mission and risk-to-force, mitigations, residual risk, signposts). Colab · exports a Word/Markdown memo.
Capstone F — The decision: the convergence map, the joint risk analysis, the residual-risk recommendation to your named authority, and the signposts — briefed in ten minutes.

Case (Schilling-style)

Capstone brief-out: each participant defends a real (abstracted) convergence decision; the seminar red-teams the hidden assumption and the unmonitored signpost.

Capstone rubric

  • Uncertainty stated, not hidden (25%) — ranges with evidence quality; deep uncertainty named.
  • Technical claims are right (25%) — correct threat vocabulary and limits from Units 2–5.
  • Robust & adaptive (20%) — failure futures, signposts, a real review date.
  • Joint-process discipline (15%) — risk-to-mission vs. risk-to-force; named authority; tolerance cited.
  • Communicates to a senior audience (15%) — bottom line first; ten minutes; signable.

Grading scale follows the host program's policy. Full worksheet: capstone.md.

Policies

Classification & handling

Entirely unclassified, open sources only. Sandboxes run in commercial cloud; no CUI, PII, or work data. Capstone work uses real decisions abstracted to an unclassified, non-attributable form — the participant's responsibility.

Use of AI tools

Encouraged for readings, drafting, and analysis, with disclosure of what was used. The capstone must be the participant's own judgment; unverified AI-generated text is an error — Unit 2 explains why.

Prerequisites

None beyond a web browser and a free Google account for Colab. No coding at any point; sandboxes run with "Run all" and interact through sliders and forms.

Accessibility & alternatives

Notebooks work with keyboard and screen readers in Colab; participants without a Google account can run them locally or work in pairs from the projected session. No-code tools have equivalents.

Currency of sources

The policy baseline moves fast. Every reference is dated and labelled final, in force, draft, or under revision as of 29 Sep 2026; the instructor re-verifies before each offering.

Glossary

Agentic authority
What an AI agent can actually do — its tools × permissions × oversight. The real measure of its risk.
Architectural innovation
A change that rearranges how components connect rather than the components themselves — what most often breaks large enterprise architectures on integration (Schilling).
CBRN uplift
The concern that frontier AI could lower barriers to chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear (or cyber) misuse; governed via capability thresholds, evaluations, and access controls.
Chiponomics
The economics and geopolitics of semiconductor design and manufacturing — concentration, export controls, and supply fragility.
Cloud shared responsibility
The split of security duties between the cloud provider and the customer; gaps here, not the data centers, cause most cloud incidents.
Competence-destroying change
An innovation that makes existing skills and assets obsolete rather than building on them — e.g., post-quantum replacing RSA/ECC (Schilling).
Contested / permissive spectrum
Whether you can rely on the electromagnetic spectrum (permissive) or must operate through jamming and denial (non-permissive/contested).
Data-center concentration
Risk from the physical clustering of compute, power, and cooling — distinct from the cloud software model that runs on it.
Deep uncertainty
When parties cannot agree on the probabilities, the models, or how to value outcomes — the state most emerging-tech decisions are in.
Harvest now, decrypt later
Recording encrypted data today to decrypt once a quantum computer exists; why exposure of long-lived data has already begun.
Inter-satellite / on-orbit link
Direct communication between satellites in orbit rather than through the ground — resilient, but its own attack surface.
LEO
Low-Earth orbit — where most new, proliferated satellite constellations operate; increasingly congested and contested.
Operating-design-domain (ODD)
The conditions an autonomous system was built and validated for; most systems cannot tell when they have left it.
OT / ICS / PLC
Operational technology, industrial control systems, and the programmable logic controllers that move physical things — where a cyberattack has physical consequences.
PNT
Position, navigation, and timing (e.g., GPS) — a hidden dependency of nearly everything, and a prime target for jamming and spoofing.
Prompt injection
Instructions hidden in content an AI reads (a document, a web page) that hijack its behavior — direct or indirect.
Risk to mission / risk to force
The two risks the joint force weighs separately: to accomplishing the mission, and to the people and equipment (CJCSM 3105.01).
Robust decision
The option that performs acceptably across the widest range of futures, rather than the one that is best if a single forecast holds.
S-curve
The pattern of slow-then-fast-then-plateau technology improvement; a way to judge maturity and how much uncertainty remains (Schilling).
Signpost
A pre-chosen, monitorable signal that a decision's assumptions are failing and it must be revisited.
